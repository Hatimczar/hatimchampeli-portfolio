Most Apple ecosystem rollouts don't fail on the technical setup. DEP, ABM, and JAMF Pro are mature, well-documented tools — the failure points are almost always process gaps that show up weeks after go-live.

## 1. DEP enrollment gaps

Devices purchased outside the sanctioned procurement channel — a manager buying a MacBook directly from Apple retail, a device transferred from another entity — never make it into Apple Business Manager. They enroll as unsupervised, unmanaged devices, invisible to your MDM until someone notices six months later during an audit.

The fix isn't technical. It's a procurement policy: every Apple device purchase routes through one channel, tied to your ABM organization ID, with no exceptions. I write that policy down and get it signed before touching a single JAMF profile.

## 2. Profile drift

JAMF configuration profiles get edited in place over time — a Wi-Fi certificate renewed, a VPN payload tweaked, a restriction loosened for one team and never reverted. Eighteen months in, nobody can tell you what a "standard" device configuration actually looks like anymore.

I treat configuration profiles the way engineers treat infrastructure: versioned, documented, and changed through a deliberate process, not ad hoc edits in the JAMF console.

## 3. The skipped step: change management

The rollout that gets remembered as "the annoying one" almost never had a technical problem. It had no communication plan. Users found out their machine was getting re-enrolled, encrypted, or restricted on the day it happened, not two weeks before with a clear explanation of why.

A two-week notice, a one-page FAQ, and a named point of contact for issues solves more support tickets than any amount of additional QA on the configuration itself.

---

None of this is exotic. It's the same lesson every enterprise IT rollout eventually learns: the technology is rarely the hard part.
